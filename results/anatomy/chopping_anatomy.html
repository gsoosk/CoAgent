Chopping anatomy

The benchmark results say whether certified chopping ends in a safe state. This experiment records the decisions that get it there. It shows where a workflow is cut, where a cut is taken back, and what happens when an agent changes its plan after part of it has committed.

Chopping on the benchmark workloads

The same twelve workloads used to compare the protocols, unmodified, run again with a recorder on the certifier. It shows where each workload is cut, where a cut is taken back, and what happens when a real agent reaches for something its plan never declared.

What was run

Five scenarios, each built to trigger one decision. Each runs once with scripted agents, so the walkthrough below is repeatable, and five times with real model-driven agents (), where nothing is scripted. The certifier is not modified. A wrapper records the plan it was given, the graph it built, every lock it held and every ruling it made. Each ruling is then compared with a brute-force check that enumerates every interleaving of the pieces.

When does a replan stop being repairable?

The fourth scenario was repaired with scripted agents and refused with real ones. One knob explains it. A always changes its plan at the same moment, 9 seconds in. What varies is how long B waits before its first write. If B's first piece has committed by then, B's future can no longer be regrouped and the replan is refused. If it has not, both futures are merged and the replan goes through.

What this does and does not show

It shows the mechanism doing what the theory says on five small cases, with every ruling confirmed by enumeration. It does not show how often each case arises in real workloads. Each scenario has two agents, and the timeline uses scripted step durations. The real-agent trials confirm which decision is reached; they are five per scenario.
