Abstract
Concurrent AI agents read and write shared application state through tools, and they interleave badly. Wrapping each
tool call in a transaction does not make an agent's intent atomic, while holding a transaction across an entire reason-
and-act workflow serializes long intervals of private computation. Transaction chopping is the classical answer to
exactly this tension, but it assumes the transaction programs are fixed before execution. Agents are not fixed: they
revise their plans after observing tool results, by which time earlier pieces have already committed and their effects
are visible.
We study the resulting problem, which we call prefix-aware recertification: deciding whether a mid-flight plan
revision can be admitted when the past can no longer be rechopped. Our central result is a complete characterization.
Because coarsening never introduces an SC cycle and sealing each workflow's uncommitted remainder into a single
piece is the coarsest repair available after commitment, a revision is admissible under future-only repair if and only if
the sealed plan is SC-cycle-free. The corollary is a genuine impossibility result: when the residual cycle passes
through a piece that has already committed, no future-only repair exists, and quiescing every other workflow does
not help. We also show admission decisions must be serialized, since two revisions that are individually admissible
can be jointly unsafe.
We implement the mechanism in a runtime that mediates every tool access, enforces certified footprints fail-closed,
executes pieces under deadlock-free strict two-phase locking, models predicate reads as ranges so phantoms are
visible to certification, and commits each piece together with its durable progress record. Bounded exhaustive
verification against a brute-force serializability oracle covers 4,352 static plans, 3,000 revision scenarios and 2,000
range scenarios with zero unsound admissions and zero violations of the characterization. On six workloads against
four equivalent-guarantee baselines, certified chopping is up to 7.4x faster than a whole-workflow database
transaction and 2.4x faster than whole-workflow two-phase locking where the structure permits chopping, and
correctly collapses to whole-workflow locking where it does not, while unchecked chopping violates serializability
and application invariants in every seed of three workloads. Two negative results are central: only 4 to 37 percent of
mid-flight escalations into shared state are admissible, and certification cost can exceed the execution savings it
enables. A small study with real language-model agents shows four concurrent agents losing three of four updates
without certified boundaries, and none with them.

1. Introduction
Consider four agents that each reserve one unit of a shared resource. Each reads the counter, reasons briefly about
whether to proceed, and writes back the incremented value. We ran exactly this with four concurrent language-model
agents against a shared database through a mediated tool interface. All four read zero, all four wrote one, and the
final counter was one instead of four: three lost updates, with no agent behaving incorrectly in isolation. The
anomaly is not a reasoning failure; it is a concurrency-control failure, and no amount of prompt engineering removes
it.
The obvious fix is to make the whole workflow a transaction. That works and is a strong baseline, but it holds
conflicting resources across the agent's reasoning, which is the expensive part. In our measurements a whole-
workflow database transaction is the slowest safe policy on every workload we tried, between 3.1x and 7.6x slower
than the best safe alternative. The opposite fix, treating each tool call as its own transaction, is fast and wrong.

Transaction chopping resolves this tension for classical workloads by splitting a transaction into pieces that may
execute and commit independently, provided a static graph over the pieces contains no cycle mixing sibling and
conflict edges [1]. The theory is mature, and recent agent systems have independently converged on transaction-
shaped abstractions for tool use, scheduling and recovery [7, 8, 9, 11, 12]. Applying chopping to agents is therefore
not, by itself, a contribution.
What actually breaks when agents replan
The classical theorem assumes the set of transaction programs is known before execution. An agent violates this
assumption in a specific, consequential way: it decides what to do next after seeing what it just read. A support agent
reads an order, and only then discovers that the resolution requires touching a shared gift-card balance. By that point
the read has committed and its result has been returned. The plan the certifier approved is no longer the plan being
executed.
One can recertify the revised plan, and that is sound. The difficulty is what to do when recertification fails. Before
execution, a failed certification is always repairable: merge pieces until, in the limit, each workflow is a single piece
with no sibling edges. After a prefix has committed, that repair is unavailable, because merging a committed piece
with a future one is not a physical operation. The question is whether some weaker repair always exists. We show it
does not, and we characterize exactly when it does.
Contributions
1. We formalize prefix-aware recertification: admitting a plan revision against a batch in which some pieces have
already committed, and where only the uncommitted suffix may be rechopped.
2. We prove a complete characterization (Theorem 2): a revision is admissible under future-only repair if and only if
sealing every workflow's uncommitted remainder into one piece yields an SC-cycle-free plan. This gives a cheap,
exact admissibility test and an algorithm that returns the finest certified chopping it can find.
3. We prove the corresponding impossibility result (Corollary 1) and show that waiting for concurrent workflows to
finish does not rescue an inadmissible revision, because committed pieces retain both their sibling and their conflict
edges. We also show admission must be serialized (Theorem 3).
4. We implement a runtime that makes the certificate's assumptions true rather than assumed: mediated access, fail-
closed footprint enforcement, range footprints for predicate reads, deadlock-free piece-level strict two-phase locking,
and atomic commit of each piece with its progress record.
5. We verify the mechanism exhaustively against brute-force ground truth, and evaluate it on six workloads against
four equivalent-guarantee baselines with real threads and real wall-clock time, including crash injection, recovery,
and a real-agent enforcement study.
We are explicit about what we do not claim. We do not claim a new isolation theorem for static choppings, a general
ACID protocol for agents, or that this is the first transactional treatment of agent tool use. Our contribution is the
revision-admission problem and its solution, plus an implementation and evaluation honest enough to show where
the approach does not pay.

2. Model
An intent is the unit an application wants to be atomic and isolated: cancel this order and refund the customer. An
intent is not an agent. Several agents can cooperate on one intent, and one long-lived agent can execute many intents,
so the transaction identifier follows the delegated work rather than the process. In this paper each intent is one
ordered workflow; distributed, internally parallel intents are future work.
A batch B is a finite set of workflow invocations. Workflow w has a plan, an ordered list of pieces, and each piece
groups consecutive steps. A step performs mediated tool operations. Pieces of one workflow execute in order, and
each piece commits before the next begins. Distinct invocations of the same program are distinct vertices; a single
representative instance would miss self-interference between two concurrent invocations of the same program.
Each piece carries a declared footprint: a set of accesses, each an exact key or a half-open key range, marked read or
write. A range models a predicate query, and a write to a key inside a declared range conflicts with a read of that

range, which is what makes phantom insertions visible to certification. Two accesses conflict when their items
overlap and at least one is a write.

The SC graph over a plan is undirected. Its vertices are pieces. An S edge joins every pair of pieces of the same
invocation, so each workflow's pieces form a clique. A C edge joins conflicting pieces of different invocations. The
forbidden object is a simple cycle containing at least one S edge and at least one C edge. Pure S cycles and pure C
cycles are permitted; a mixed closed walk is not sufficient. We detect mixed simple cycles by computing vertex-
biconnected edge blocks and rejecting any block containing both labels, which is equivalent because any two edges
of a biconnected block lie on a common simple cycle.
The runtime obligations are substantive and we implement all of them. Each piece must itself execute serializably;
several atomic tool calls under one label are not an atomic piece. Program order between pieces must be respected.
Declared footprints must bound actual accesses, enforced rather than trusted. Pieces aborted by contention must be
retried to completion. These are the classical execution rules [1] restated for a tool runtime.
What a certificate does and does not buy
A certificate is a statement about isolation under a declared model. It is not atomicity: independently committed
pieces leave a visible prefix if the process dies, which we demonstrate and then repair with durable continuation. It is
not consistency: serializability preserves invariants only if each workflow is individually correct. It is not real-time
ordering: conflict serializability does not imply strict serializability. It says nothing about observations outside the
modeled state, including messages already sent. Irreversible external effects remain irreversible; a graph cannot
create rollback for a service that does not offer it.

3. Prefix-aware recertification
Let plan P be certified and executing. The frontier phi records, for each workflow w, how many of its pieces have
committed. Workflow r proposes to replace its uncommitted suffix with a new sequence, giving a revised plan P'.
The runtime must decide whether to admit P', and if the proposed chopping is too fine, whether it can be repaired.
3.1 Soundness of recertification
   Theorem 1 (Inherited isolation). If P' is SC-cycle-free, its committed prefix is identical to the prefix actually
   executed, and the runtime satisfies the execution rules, then the resulting workflow history is conflict-serializable.
Proof. The pieces that already ran are, by construction, exactly the prefix pieces of P', with the footprints they
declared, and enforcement guarantees their actual accesses were inside those footprints. The observed execution is
therefore a legal history of the chopping P' under the execution rules. The classical chopping theorem [1] applies to
P' and yields serial equivalence with respect to the workflows of P'. This is an application of established theory to a
revised plan, not a new isolation theorem.
The force of Theorem 1 is that recertification must cover the whole batch including committed pieces. Certifying
only the new suffix is unsound, because it discards exactly the conflicts that close a cycle through the past.
3.2 What repairs remain after commitment
Before execution, a rejected chopping is repaired by merging pieces. After commitment, only the uncommitted suffix
may be regrouped. Call P'' a future-only coarsening of P' at phi when the first phi(w) pieces of every workflow are
unchanged and the remaining pieces are grouped into contiguous runs. Define seal(P', phi) as the future-only
coarsening that merges each workflow's entire remainder into one piece.
   Lemma 1 (Sealing is coarsest). Every future-only coarsening of P' at phi is a refinement of seal(P', phi).
Proof. Both agree on the committed prefix. Sealing places each workflow's whole remainder in a single piece, and
any other future-only coarsening partitions that same remainder into contiguous runs, which is precisely a refinement
of the single piece.
   Lemma 2 (Cycles persist under refinement). If a chopping contains a mixed simple cycle, so does every
   refinement of it.

                                                Preliminary research draft | 3
This is the classical non-removability property of chopping [1]: splitting pieces can only add S edges and redistribute
C edges among the parts, so an existing mixed cycle survives. We check it empirically as well, over 1,023
refinements drawn from 258 scenarios whose sealing is uncertifiable, with no counterexample.
   Theorem 2 (Admissibility characterization). A revision is admissible under future-only coarsening if and only
   if seal(P', phi) is SC-cycle-free.
Proof. If sealing is cycle-free, sealing is itself a certified future-only coarsening, so the revision is admissible.
Conversely, suppose sealing contains a mixed simple cycle. By Lemma 1 every future-only coarsening is a
refinement of sealing, and by Lemma 2 every such refinement contains a mixed simple cycle. Hence no future-only
coarsening certifies.
Theorem 2 turns admissibility into a single cheap test on the coarsest candidate, and it tells the runtime that if that
test fails, searching the exponentially many intermediate choppings is pointless. When the test passes, we still want
the finest certified plan rather than the coarsest, so the algorithm certifies the proposal directly, and only if that fails
runs a guided merge restricted to indices at or beyond the frontier.
   Corollary 1 (Impossibility of future-only repair). If every mixed simple cycle in seal(P', phi) passes through an
   S edge incident to a committed piece, then no future-only repair exists, and the runtime must refuse the revision
   or compensate the committed prefix.
The practically important part of Corollary 1 is that waiting does not help. One might hope that letting all other
workflows finish would dissolve the conflict. It does not: a workflow that has fully committed still contributes its S
clique and its C edges, because those interleavings really happened. Concretely, suppose W0's committed piece read
x and W1 committed a piece that wrote x and read y. If W0 now proposes to write y, the cycle W0-prefix, W1, W0-
suffix is mixed and passes through W0's committed read. Our verifier confirms this instance is rejected, that the
single available future-only chopping does not certify, and that the plan genuinely admits a non-serializable
execution. The runtime's options are to refuse the revision, or to run a compensating action, which is an application-
level obligation rather than a graph property.
3.3 Admission must be serialized
   Theorem 3 (Serialized admission). Two revisions that are each admissible against a common snapshot may be
   jointly inadmissible, so admission decisions must be evaluated and applied against a single serialized plan
   snapshot.
Proof by counterexample, machine-checked in the artifact. W0 has committed a read of x and W1 a read of y. W0
proposes to write y and W1 proposes to write x. Each revision alone is admitted. Applied together the plan is
rejected, and it does in fact admit a non-serializable execution. Our runtime therefore evaluates and installs revisions
under a single admission lock, which also fixes the frontier used for sealing.
3.4 Algorithm
Admission proceeds in three steps. Certify the proposed revised plan; if it passes, install it unchanged. Otherwise test
seal(P', phi); if that fails, refuse, returning the witness cycle and the workflows it implicates so the application can
choose compensation or an alternative plan. Otherwise run guided coarsening restricted to the uncommitted suffix,
repairing every mixed block in each pass and choosing, per workflow, the interval whose merge adds the least
footprint-duration area. Each pass strictly reduces the piece count, so the procedure terminates, and by Theorem 2 it
must succeed once sealing has certified.

4. Runtime
The runtime is about 1,470 lines of Python over SQLite in write-ahead-logging mode. Its job is to make the
certificate's hypotheses true.
Mediation and fail-closed enforcement. Every read, scan, write and delete passes through an adapter that checks the
access against the manifest of the currently executing piece. An access outside the manifest is refused before it
reaches the database, buffered writes are revalidated against the manifest at commit, and the piece is aborted.
Enforcement does not depend on the agent's cooperation, which matters because a model-generated description of a

                                                  Preliminary research draft | 4
tool is not evidence about that tool's effects. The trust boundary is explicit: workflow bodies that run in-process are
trusted not to bypass the adapter and reach the database directly, whereas the agent driving them is not. The real-
agent study in Section 6.7 exercises the untrusted case, where the caller is a separate process that can only reach state
through the mediated tool.
Piece isolation. A piece acquires locks for its entire declared footprint atomically, in one wait, before executing.
Because nothing is acquired incrementally while holding locks, the lock manager is deadlock-free by construction,
for chopped pieces and for whole-workflow conservative two-phase locking alike. Read and write modes are
distinguished, and ranges lock intervals so that an insert into a scanned range is blocked.
Durable continuation. Each piece's buffered writes and the workflow's progress record, including its local variables,
are written in a single database transaction. A crash inside that transaction rolls back both, so pieces are atomic. A
crash after it leaves a durable marker from which recovery replays the remaining pieces forward. This is the recovery
obligation that chopping requires and that a certificate alone does not provide.
Baselines. To keep the comparison about mechanism rather than guarantees, all policies share the same store,
workflow code, footprints and reasoning delays. Whole DB transaction runs the entire workflow inside one exclusive
database transaction. Whole-workflow 2PL holds the union footprint for the workflow's lifetime and commits once.
Whole-workflow OCC runs unlocked, then validates every read version and re-executes every declared range scan
before committing, so it is not vulnerable to phantoms, and retries the whole workflow on conflict. Unchecked
chopping executes the proposed fine pieces with no certification and is included as an unsafe control.
Correctness oracle. We do not judge correctness by final state alone. Every mediated operation is recorded, and after
each run we build the workflow-level serialization graph from the actual recorded conflicts and test it for cycles.
Two details make the oracle sound rather than approximate. Write events are published when the piece's transaction
applies them, so the recorded order is the order the database actually took rather than the order the workflow buffered
them; and the events of an aborted optimistic attempt are retracted so they cannot contribute spurious edges.
Application invariants are checked separately.
Plan-state concurrency. Plan installation, the frontier and the set of currently executing pieces are guarded by one
lock. A piece that is executing is treated as immutable exactly like one that has committed, so a concurrent admission
can never regroup a piece that another thread is in the middle of running. An earlier version of this runtime serialized
only the admission decision itself and had a race here; the verifier now exercises the concurrent path directly.

These results are bounds, not proofs of unbounded correctness. They cover small batches, a two-item alphabet for the
static sweep, and syntactic read/write conflicts. They do not establish that real tool manifests are sound, which is an
assumption the runtime enforces but cannot originate.

The claim workload takes a shared counter, reasons privately, then writes a private result. Certification keeps all 96
pieces and delivers 7.35x over the whole database transaction and 2.35x over whole-workflow two-phase locking,
with p95 latency falling from 1.96 s to 0.12 s. This is the case chopping exists for: the shared dependency is short

The budget, retail and calendar workloads are the honest negative result. Each has a read-modify-write or scan-then-
insert straddling the reasoning gap, so certification correctly merges the pieces and the policy becomes whole-

The escalate workload, where two thirds of workflows revise their plan mid-flight, is where prefix-aware
recertification earns its place: 6.24x versus 3.04x for two-phase locking and 4.51x for optimistic concurrency control,
with no violations, while the unchecked variant fails in all 10 seeds.
6.2 Enforcement against a dishonest manifest
We ran six workflows whose declared footprint claims a private write but whose code writes shared state. With
enforcement disabled, every write lands, the shared counter is corrupted and the recorded history is not serializable.

Between 4 and 37 percent of revisions are admissible; the rest are refused because the residual cycle passes through a

question correctly and cheaply, and it never admits an unsafe revision, but the answer is frequently no. An agent
architecture that escalates into shared state after committing a prefix should expect refusal to be the common case,
and should be designed to either declare the contingent footprint up front, defer commitment until the plan is settled,
or provide compensation. The whole-workflow baselines avoid the problem by reserving the conservative union
footprint in advance, which is precisely the cost chopping is trying to avoid.

7. Discussion and limitations
The result we would emphasize to a system designer is not the speedup. It is that the admissibility question for mid-
flight revisions has a clean answer, that the answer is often no, and that no runtime cleverness changes it once effects
are visible. This argues for declaring contingent footprints before committing anything, and for treating the point of
first visible write as a commitment boundary in agent design, not merely an implementation detail.
Threats to validity. Reasoning is a calibrated delay in the throughput experiments; real inference has different
variance, caching behaviour and cost structure, and an optimistic policy that discards a long generation pays far more
than our model charges it. The workloads are small, hand-built and chosen to expose specific graph structures; they
are not a measured distribution of production agent traffic, and we do not quantify how often useful safe cuts occur
in the wild. Footprints are supplied by the workload rather than inferred from real tool implementations, so we
evaluate enforcement of manifests, not their derivation, and in-process workflow bodies are trusted not to bypass the
adapter. The store is a single local SQLite database: there are no network partitions, no distributed participants, no
irreversible external effects, and no multi-service commit. Verification is bounded and syntactic. The real-agent
study has four agents per arm, one model and one task shape, and arm A's exact lost-update count depends on timing.
Relation to prior work. Chopping, semantic conflicts including return values, and the non-removability of cycles
under refinement are classical [1], as is the separation of serializability from recoverability [2]. Transaction chains,
Callas and IC3 combine static analysis with runtime ordering or dependency tracking to admit executions a static no-
cycle test would reject [3, 4, 5]; our criterion is deliberately more conservative and correspondingly cheaper, and
their mechanisms suggest a natural way to weaken our refusal rate. Chopping under parallel snapshot isolation
targets a different correctness condition and shows how the permitted observer population changes safety [6]. On the
agent side, Atomix provides transactional tool use with declared effects and settlement, where the integration layer
chooses transaction granularity [7]; OptiMA schedules multi-agent transactions treated as indivisible [8]; CoAgent
orders agents and repairs conflicts semantically, assuming notified agents self-heal correctly [9]; concurrency
anomalies in multi-agent systems have been formally studied [10]; SagaLLM applies compensation-based
decomposition while explicitly relaxing ACID [11]; and Mnemosyne admits untrusted proposals through a
deterministic gate [12]. Automatic read-set reconstruction [14] and adaptive control for agent-generated SQL [13]
are complementary. What we did not find in that body of work, and what this paper supplies, is the treatment of plan
revision over an already-committed prefix, with an admissibility characterization and an impossibility result. That is
a search finding, not a priority claim.
Future work. The most valuable next step is to relax refusal using the observed past. Our criterion is static and
therefore quantifies over all executions of the revised plan, including executions that the already-fixed history has
ruled out. A hybrid criterion that combines the directed dependency graph of the committed prefix with static
conflicts over the future should admit strictly more revisions while remaining sound, and Theorem 2 gives the exact

approximation, extending the runtime across multiple services with a defined commit protocol, and evaluating on
adapted concurrent versions of published agent benchmarks such as tau-bench retail and WorkBench [16, 17], whose
isolated task runs do not by themselves create the contention this problem is about.

8. Conclusion
Agents interleave, and the classical answer to interleaving assumes plans that agents do not have. We identified the
resulting problem, characterized it completely, and showed that the characterization has teeth: after effects become
visible, a large fraction of plan revisions cannot be made safe by any regrouping of the future. We built a runtime
that enforces rather than assumes the certificate's hypotheses, verified the mechanism exhaustively against brute-
force ground truth, and measured it against baselines with matched guarantees, reporting both the cases where it wins
clearly and the cases where the right answer is not to chop at all.
